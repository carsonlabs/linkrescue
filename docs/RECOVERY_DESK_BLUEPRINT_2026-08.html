The Attribution Leak Thesis

Every competitor sells software that watches links it already knows about. Nobody sells labour to the publisher side. That is the whole business — and one number in your own scan data proves it.

The read, before the strategy

Three things are true at once, and the plan has to hold all three.

One: the product is not the problem. Production is healthy, the database is recovered, the acceptance test passed, and the public copy is claim-safe. Nothing in the next fifty paragraphs is blocked on engineering.

Two: you are sitting on better proof than your competitors publish. The June scan is original primary research on 50 real publisher sites. Lasso and Geniuslink have marketing pages. You have a dataset with a stated methodology.

Three: you have never performed a single distribution action. Zero posts, zero sends. Every strategic word below is worth exactly nothing until that changes.

The one finding that reorganises everything

Of 597 affiliate issues found in the June scan, 569 were lost tracking parameters — not broken links. Those links return 200 OK. The page loads. The reader buys. The commission goes nowhere.

A broken-link checker marks that link healthy. A VA clicking through marks it healthy. Google Analytics marks it healthy. It is invisible to every instrument the publisher currently owns, and it is 95% of the affiliate-specific damage you detect.

The line to memorise

"A plugin monitors the links you gave it. Your problem is the links you never gave it."

Five real objections, and the exact counter

1 — "I already have Lasso / Broken Link Checker"

Counter: "Both are good at what they do — they watch the links they manage. My snapshot ran against your published HTML, not your plugin database, so it sees the raw hrefs in posts from before you installed it. If those two sets match, you'll see a near-empty report and you should keep your money. On the sites I scanned, they never matched."

Note the move: you are not attacking the tool, you are redefining the scope of the audit so the tool is genuinely out of frame. And you offer them the exit, which is what makes it credible.

2 — "How much money am I actually losing?" hardest

This is the one that will kill deals, because your claim-safety rule forbids you from answering it the way they want. Do not fudge it. Convert the question:

Counter: "I won't give you a number, and I'd be careful with anyone who does — they can't see your commission rates or your conversion data, so it's a guess with a dollar sign on it. What I can give you is exposure: N links on N pages that currently earn you nothing when someone clicks them, and which pages those are. You know your RPM better than I ever will. Multiply it yourself."

This converts a weakness into the most trustworthy thing in the conversation. In a category full of inflated ROI calculators, refusing to estimate is a differentiator. Say it out loud.

3 — "Your scan only found four things"

The real risk, and it is an operations problem, not a sales one. In the June scan 68% of sites cleared the crawl gate — 10 of 50 yielded zero data and 6 more were thin, largely from bot-blocking. A thin snapshot is worse than no snapshot: it actively tells the prospect they are fine.

Counter (pre-emptive, before you ever send a thin report): "Your site blocks automated requests, which is normally a good thing. It means my free snapshot can only see N pages and you should not read anything into that number. If you want real coverage I'd need you to allow my crawler for 48 hours, or I do the archive section by hand as part of a Sprint."

Bot-blocking is a qualification signal, not a failure — a site defended enough to block you is a site with something worth defending. Route it to a conversation, never to a report.

4 — "I'll have my VA do it"

Counter: "For the dead links, honestly, yes — a VA can do that, and if that's the whole job you should use them. The part they can't do is the one that matters: when a link still works but drops your tag somewhere in the redirect chain, it looks perfect to a person clicking it. Half the links I flagged in my study were that. Your VA will mark every one of them green."

Conceding the commodity half is what makes the second half land.

5 — "Who are you? I see no case studies."

You removed the fabricated testimonials, which was correct, and it leaves you naked. Do not fill the space with vagueness — fill it with method.

Counter: "None yet — you'd be early, and I'd rather say that than invent one. What I have instead is the study: 50 publisher sites, 683 pages, 6,550 links checked in June, methodology published. That's what I'm using to find people worth talking to. If the work isn't useful to you, don't pay for it."

Then add the structural fix: make your first three Sprints cheap in exchange for a named, quotable result. Price is recoverable. A permanent absence of proof is not.

The outbound engine

Read this before the templates

Your own operating record says eight consecutive lanes stalled at the identical step: contacting a stranger. You lifted that constraint yourself on 2026-08-07, so the templates below are written as asked and are yours to send.

But do not make cold email the engine. Build it as the second motion behind a channel that does not require you to initiate with a stranger — because on nine attempts, that is the step that has never once survived contact with a Tuesday. The board already has the first action staged and unfired: one LinkedIn post, from your own profile, with the campaign URL that is already verified live. Post it, then run outbound into the audience it surfaces.

Every template obeys three rules: the evidence is the offer, the price is never in message one, and the exit is always visible. You are selling a look at the finding — nothing else.

Template 1 — independent publisher

to: publisher subject: 3 links on [site.com] that load fine but don't track

Hi [name] —

I ran a technical scan across 50 affiliate sites in June to study how outbound links fail over time. [site.com] was in the set.

The thing that surprised me wasn't dead links. It was links that still return a normal 200 — the page loads, the reader can buy — but the tracking parameter gets dropped somewhere in the redirect chain. On your [specific post title] post I logged [N] of those.

Those don't show up in a broken link checker, and they look completely fine if you click them yourself.

Want the list? It's about a page, it's free, and there's nothing to sign up for. If it's useful we can talk about the rest of the archive; if not, delete this and no hard feelings.

[Carson]

Why this works: the subject line is a factual claim about their property, not a benefit statement. The mechanism is explained in one sentence so the reader learns something even if they never reply. The ask is "want the list," which costs them nothing and is not a meeting.

Do not send without a real post title and a real count. Generic personalisation is worse than none — it announces a template.

Template 2 — agency

to: head of content / SEO director subject: when a client's affiliate revenue drops and it isn't SEO

Hi [name] —

Question for you, since you run content sites for clients.

When a client's affiliate revenue falls and the rankings haven't moved, how do you currently rule out the mechanical causes — merchant replatforms, changed deep-link formats, tracking parameters dropped in a redirect?

I ask because I scanned 50 publisher sites in June and the attribution failures were roughly ten times more common than outright dead links. Every one of them returns a healthy 200, so a normal site audit passes them.

I run those audits as fixed-scope work for agencies — white-labelled, your logo, delivered as a client-ready document. Most of my agency conversations start with one client site as a test.

If it's useful I'll run a free snapshot on any one client property this week and send you what it finds. No charge and no obligation — I'd mainly like to know whether the output is something you could actually hand to a client.

[Carson]

Why this works: opens on their process, not your product — a genuine question an operator can answer. "Ten times more common" is your own measured data, not a market statistic. The free snapshot is framed as you asking them for a quality judgement, which reverses the status of the ask.

The margin line goes in the reply, not here. Message one must not read as a reseller pitch.

Template 3 — re-engagement after a free snapshot

to: lead who ran a snapshot and went quiet subject: re-checked [site.com] — two of those moved

You ran a snapshot on [site.com] a few weeks back. I re-ran it this morning out of curiosity.

[N] of the issues are still open, and [N] new ones appeared — the [merchant] links on [section] now redirect to their homepage rather than the product page.

That's the part I find most people underestimate: this isn't a backlog you clear once. Merchants change things on their schedule, not yours.

Why this works: it is the only follow-up that carries new information rather than new pressure. It also demonstrates the recurring nature of the problem through evidence instead of argument — which is the exact case for the Monitoring Desk, made without mentioning it.

Operational note: this template is why the Day-30 re-verify in §04 exists. The follow-up writes itself only if the re-scan is already scheduled.
Sequence discipline

One follow-up after four business days, then stop — as your own distribution kit already specifies. Do not build a five-touch sequence. At this volume the marginal reply is not worth the reputational cost, and a solo operator cannot defend a large send.

04

The $499 Sprint: delivery under 2.5 hours

$499 at 2.5 hours is roughly $200/hour, which works — but only if the time is defended. The margin does not die in the analysis. It dies in unqualified sites, scope creep, and hand-written reports. Three fixed rules protect it.

The three margin rules
  • Never start a Sprint before the crawl gate passes. 32% of sites in your study yielded thin or no data. An unqualified site is a five-hour Sprint at best and a refund at worst.
  • The report is assembled, never written. Eight fixed modules, same order every time. Your judgement goes into module 4 and nowhere else.
  • You deliver a repair map. You never touch their site. The moment you edit a client's WordPress you own every subsequent bug on it, and the 2.5 hours becomes unbounded.

05

The ladder is broken at step two

Free Snapshot → $499 Sprint → $149/mo Desk looks clean and contains one structural flaw that will cost you most of your recurring revenue if you do not fix it before the first sale.

The audit paradox

The Sprint's job is to leave the site clean. You then ask the customer to pay $149/month to be told, repeatedly, that it is still clean. You have engineered your own churn: the better the Sprint, the weaker the case for the Desk.

Monitoring sold as watching is insurance, and solo operators cannot sell insurance. Monitoring sold as work performed each cycle is a service. Same activity, entirely different renewal conversation.

Three packaging changes

1 — The Sprint fee credits against the Desk

"$499 for the Sprint. If you start the Desk within 30 days, the $499 credits against your first three months." The customer decides once, at peak enthusiasm, rather than twice — and the second decision is the one that historically never happens. Zero additional operational work; it is a sentence in the proposal.

Cash effect: $499 + ($149 × 3 − $499 credit) is worse in month one and materially better by month four, and it converts your hardest sale into a discount rather than a new purchase.

2 — Invert the ladder for agencies

For Persona B, stop selling the Sprint first. Sell the $499/mo Agency Desk covering five client sites, with the first client Sprint as the paid trial. An agency does not want a one-time audit — it wants a repeatable line item it can mark up. Leading with the one-time offer trains them to treat you as a project vendor, which is the low-LTV outcome.

Same two products. Reversed order. This is the single highest-leverage change in the document: it moves your best buyer from a $499 transaction to a $5,988/year relationship without any new delivery capability.

3 — Reprice the Desk on output, and add annual prepay

Redefine the monthly unit as a recovery cycle, not a subscription: each month delivers a re-scan, a verified issue list, replacement targets for anything newly dead, and a one-page delta. There is always an artifact, even in a quiet month — because merchants change things constantly and a quiet month is itself a finding worth stating.

Then offer annual at ten months for twelve. For a solo operator this is not a discount, it is working capital plus twelve months of eliminated churn admin, and it removes the monthly "is this still worth it" moment that kills small retainers.

The conversion mechanism, concretely

Do not "follow up about monitoring." Build the conversion into the delivery so it happens on its own:

  1. Module 8 sets the appointment. Every Sprint report includes a free Day-30 re-verification. This is stated as quality assurance — you are checking your own repair queue worked — and it is true.
  2. Day 30, you re-scan. Twenty minutes, automated. On any real publisher archive it will surface new findings, because merchant-side change is continuous and exogenous. Your own study is the evidence: 9.1% attribution failure was a steady-state rate, not a backlog.
  3. You send the delta, not a pitch. "Your queue got executed — 22 of 27 resolved. Four new ones appeared since: [merchant] changed their deep-link format on the 14th."
  4. The customer reaches the conclusion themselves. The recurrence is now demonstrated on their own property with their own merchants. The ask is one line: "This is what the Desk does every month. Want me to keep going?"

You are not arguing that link rot recurs. You are showing it recurring, on their site, thirty days after they paid you to fix it. That is the entire retainer motion and it costs twenty minutes.

What to cut

  • The self-serve SaaS tiers. The repo still carries a $29/$79 three-tier config with a revenue_estimates feature flag and "estimated revenue protected" emails. That contradicts the claim-safety work and splits your positioning across two incompatible businesses. Pick the service.

The honest bottom line

The thesis is sound: you have a real detection advantage on a failure mode no incumbent covers, a buyer who already spends money in this category, and original proof. The delivery model fits inside 2.5 hours and the agency motion clears $6k a year per yes.

None of that has ever been the constraint. The constraint is a single post that has been written, verified and unfired since the eighteenth. The strategy is finished. The publish button is the whole remaining risk.
